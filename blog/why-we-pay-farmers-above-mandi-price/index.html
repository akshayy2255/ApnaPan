Why we pay 22% above the mandi — and how the maths still works

Every spice brand says 'farm-direct'. Here is our actual cost sheet: what we pay, what we save, and where the premium comes from.

There is a version of 'farm-direct' where a company buys a truckload at the mandi, takes the photo opportunity at the farm gate and puts the farmer's face on a label. We have been asked to do that. We do not, because the arithmetic of buying at the farm gate actually works — but only if you are honest about all four numbers in it.

Number 1: what we pay

In 2025 our average purchase price across chilli, coriander, turmeric, dal, groundnut, amla and mango midi was 22% above the prevailing local mandi rate for the same grade. Our chilli rate to Rudrappa in Haveri was ₹138 a kilo against a mandi rate of ₹112. That premium is not charity and it is not marketing. It is the price of getting a specific grade — under 10% moisture, cleaned, sorted, and delivered in one lot instead of being mixed with three other farmers' bags.

Number 2: what the middleman actually costs us

When we began, we bought from commission agents. Between the agent's commission, the transporter's cut, the 'gunnysack shrink' that never quite added up, and the rejections after loading, we were paying 26–31% over the farm-gate rate to receive a grade we never fully controlled. Today we pay 22% over the farm gate and receive exactly the grade we asked for, weighed on a scale the farmer can see. The premium is roughly the difference we no longer give away to three people in the middle.

Number 3: what quality does to the rest of the factory

This is the number most people miss. Clean, dry, properly graded produce does not just taste better — it changes the whole production line. Moisture under 10% means the chilli roasts evenly, which means fewer burnt batches and less rework. Sorted produce means our women are not spending hours picking stones out of a conveyor. Across 2025, our rejection and rework losses fell from 7.1% to 3.4% of input. That is worth more to us than the entire premium we pay.

A farmer who is paid properly in April will dry his crop properly in June. That is not sentiment — it is supply chain management.

Number 4: what it costs the customer

A 200g jar of our masala is ₹185. A supermarket brand's 200g sachet is ₹99–₹120 with roughly a fifth of it fillers and anti-caking agents. Our price is higher because there is nothing in the jar except spice — no starch, no salt as a bulking agent, no colour. Per serving, our masala costs about ₹9; the cheap sachet costs ₹7 and needs more of it. We are not the cheapest. We are the one where more of the rupee goes to the person who grew the crop.

What we will not do

We will not buy from a farmer at a rate below the mandi and call it 'direct sourcing'. We will not deduct for moisture after the goods have left the village. And we will not tell you we are the cheapest masala in India. We are the one that can tell you the name of the farmer, the village, and the date the money was transferred.

Written by Lakshmi Devi R., Founder for ApnaPan. Figures quoted are from our own records and are illustrative on this demonstration site.
